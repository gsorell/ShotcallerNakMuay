Training Muay Thai as a Southpaw: The Adjustments Every Left-Handed Fighter Needs

By Shotcaller Sam
August 31, 2026

Roughly one in ten people is left-handed, but almost all Muay Thai instructional content, numbering systems, and combo apps are built orthodox-first. If you're a southpaw, you've probably spent training sessions doing mental gymnastics — mirroring every "jab, cross, hook" call in real time before your body can react.

That translation delay is the exact opposite of what a reactive drill is supposed to build. Here's how to actually train Muay Thai as a southpaw, without constantly working against the material.

Why Southpaw Training Gets Overlooked

Most combo generators and timer apps are written by and for orthodox fighters, since they're the majority. Numbering systems like "1 = jab" assume a lead left hand. When a southpaw hears these calls, they have to flip lead and rear on the fly — a small tax on every single strike that adds up over a 5-round session and quietly caps how fast you can react.

The Core Stance Adjustments

As a southpaw, your lead hand and lead leg are your right side. Your power side — the one generating the heavy cross and rear kick — is your left. This isn't just a mirror image of orthodox technique; it changes how you close distance and where your power angles land.

  • Open vs. Closed Stance: Against an orthodox opponent, you're in an "open" stance — your lead legs are on the same side. This changes which angles are available compared to fighting another southpaw, where the stance is "closed."
  • The Outside Angle: Southpaws in an open stance often have easier access to the outside of an orthodox opponent's lead leg with the rear teep and rear low kick, since there's nothing blocking that lane.
  • Rear Straight Placement: Your rear cross (left hand) travels a slightly different line than an orthodox rear cross, because your hips and shoulders are rotated the opposite way. Drill it deliberately rather than assuming it's a pure mirror.

The Mistake Most Lefties Make

The most common error isn't technical — it's mental. Southpaws who learn primarily from orthodox instruction often just mirror everything 1:1 without questioning it, including footwork patterns that were designed around an orthodox fighter's angles. Pivots that create outside angles for an orthodox fighter can put a southpaw in a worse position if copied blindly. Treat every drill as something to test for your own stance, not just flip.

How Shot Caller Handles It

Flip it on, and every "Left" and "Right" in the callout engine mirrors automatically — so "Left Hook" becomes your actual lead hook instead of forcing you to translate mid-combo. It's a small switch that removes the single biggest source of hesitation in southpaw solo training.

Turn On Southpaw Mode First
